# Chapter 14 — Doing the Right Thing

**DDIA 2nd Edition, Chapter 14**

> "Feeding AI systems on the world's beauty, ugliness, and cruelty, but expecting it to reflect only the beauty is a fantasy."

## Topics

- Ethics in data systems: every system has intended and unintended consequences, datasets are about people (behavior, interests, identity), treating data with humanity and respect, human dignity as paramount
  - Engineers' responsibility: the ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct and why it is rarely applied in practice, technology as neither good nor bad in itself — what matters is how it is used and how it affects people
  - Ethics as interpretation: ethical concepts are not fixed or determinate, ethics is not a compliance checklist but a participatory and iterative process of reflection, in dialog with the people involved, with accountability for the results
- Predictive analytics: predicting weather or disease spread vs predicting reoffending, loan default, or insurance claims — decisions with direct effects on individual lives
  - Asymmetric costs and "if in doubt, say no": why organizations are cautious, systematic exclusion from jobs, air travel, insurance, rental, and financial services — the "algorithmic prison", exclusion without proof of guilt and with little chance of appeal
  - Bias and discrimination: human biases vs algorithmic ones, rules inferred from data rather than specified, opaque learned patterns, algorithms amplifying systematic bias in input data
    - Protected traits (ethnicity, age, gender, sexuality, disability, beliefs) and proxy variables: postal code or IP address as strong predictors of race in segregated neighborhoods
    - "Machine learning is like money laundering for bias": extrapolating from a discriminatory past codifies and amplifies discrimination, moral imagination as something only humans can provide — data and models as tools, not masters
  - Responsibility and accountability: who is accountable when algorithms go wrong (self-driving cars, discriminatory credit scoring), explainability under judicial review, people evading responsibility by blaming an algorithm
    - Credit scores vs ML scoring: "how did you behave in the past?" vs "how did people like you behave in the past?", stereotyping and wrong-bucket errors, near-impossible recourse for decisions based on erroneous data
    - Probabilistic outputs: correct distributions vs wrong individual cases, the danger of blind belief in the supremacy of data, positive potential (targeting aid) vs predatory uses (targeting vulnerable people with risky products)
  - Feedback loops: echo chambers from recommendation systems (stereotypes, misinformation, polarization, election impact), self-reinforcing spirals (credit scores used in hiring → joblessness → poverty → worse score), algorithmic pricing learning to collude (the German gas station study)
    - Systems thinking: analyzing the entire system including the people interacting with it, asking whether the system reinforces and amplifies existing differences or combats injustice
- Privacy and tracking: the ethics of data collection itself, user-entered data (user as customer) vs behavioral tracking as a side effect (the service takes on interests of its own)
  - Legitimate uses of tracking: search-ranking improvement, recommendations, A/B tests — vs advertising-funded services where advertisers are the actual customers and tracking serves them, not the user
  - Surveillance: the "replace data with surveillance" thought experiment, the greatest mass surveillance infrastructure ever built, internet-connected microphones in every inhabited space, corporate vs governmental data collection, surveillance knowing more about a person than they know about themselves
    - When surveillance stops looking benign: driving behavior affecting insurance premiums, health coverage tied to fitness trackers, smartwatch motion sensors revealing what you type
  - Consent and freedom of choice: whether tracking is genuinely necessary for the service, privacy policies that obscure rather than illuminate, consent without understanding is not meaningful, data about non-users who never agreed to terms, the asymmetric one-sided relationship with no negotiation
    - GDPR consent requirements: "freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous", withdrawal without detriment, clear and plain language, no pre-ticked boxes or inactivity as consent; other lawful bases including legitimate interest (e.g., fraud prevention)
    - Services "essential for basic social participation": network effects and the social cost of opting out, engagement mechanics borrowed from gambling, freedom of choice as a privilege — for the less privileged, surveillance becomes inescapable
  - Privacy and use of data: privacy as a decision right (freedom to choose what to reveal to whom), not secrecy — the "privacy is dead" fallacy, privacy rights transferred from individuals to data collectors rather than eroded
    - Companies exercising the privacy right for profit, managing perceptions of creepiness instead of reducing intrusiveness, data that is wrong, undesirable, or inappropriate and the need to plan for such failures, privacy settings vs the service's own unfettered access, the historically unprecedented scale of this transfer vs traditional governed trust relationships (doctor–patient, attorney–client)
  - Data as assets and power: "data exhaust" vs behavioral data as a form of labor, data brokers, startups valued by "eyeballs"/surveillance capabilities, governments obtaining data by secret deals, coercion, legal compulsion, or theft, personal data sold in bankruptcy
    - Data as "toxic asset", "hazardous material", "the new uranium": balancing benefits against the risk of data falling into the wrong hands, considering all possible future governments, "poor civic hygiene to install technologies that could someday facilitate a police state", scrutinizing others while avoiding scrutiny as a form of power
  - Remembering the Industrial Revolution: parallels to the information age, pollution and exploitation before safeguards, regulation raising the cost of business while benefiting society, Schneier's "data is the pollution problem of the information age, and protecting privacy is the environmental challenge"
  - Legislation and self-regulation: GDPR purpose limitation ("specified, explicit and legitimate purposes") and data minimization ("adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary") vs the big-data philosophy of maximizing collection and exploring for unforeseen purposes, weak enforcement in practice
    - Overregulation risks: medical data sharing and prevented breakthroughs, balancing opportunity with risk
    - The needed culture shift: users as humans deserving respect, dignity, and agency — not metrics to be optimized, educating users rather than keeping them in the dark, purging data as soon as it is no longer needed, minimizing collection ("data you don't have is data that can't be leaked, stolen, or compelled"), considering the societal impact of our work as part of the job

## Assignment

Design the personal-data handling architecture for a consumer product feature that depends on behavioral data — a personalization or recommendation feature backed by user activity tracking. You must decide what to collect and why, on what lawful and ethical basis, how consent is obtained meaningfully, how the data flows into derived datasets and automated decisions, how long each category of data lives, and how deletion actually works across raw logs, derived data, and backups. The chapter's core tension is your design space: the big-data instinct to collect everything and explore later runs directly against data minimization, purpose limitation, and the view of stored personal data as a hazardous asset.

1. **Theory pass** — conversational interview on predictive analytics and algorithmic decision making, bias amplification and proxy variables, accountability and recourse, feedback loops and systems thinking, tracking vs surveillance, meaningful consent, privacy as a decision right, data as a toxic asset, and data minimization vs the big-data philosophy
2. **Design pass** — produce a `DESIGN.md`: define the feature and enumerate every category of personal data it touches, decide what you collect vs deliberately do not collect and justify each against purpose limitation, choose a consent/lawful-basis model and specify how it avoids the meaningless-consent trap, identify where automated decisions affect users and what appeal/recourse path exists, trace how personal data propagates into derived datasets, define a retention schedule per data category and the mechanics of purging, specify what happens on a user's deletion request (including derived data and backups), and analyze at least one potential feedback loop or proxy-discrimination risk in your feature. Include rejected alternatives with reasoning.
3. **Implementation pass** — implement the retention and deletion mechanics as working code: a small data store (or event log plus derived views) where every record carries purpose and retention metadata, a purge job that enforces the retention schedule, and a user deletion request that verifiably propagates through raw and derived data — with a way to demonstrate that deleted data is actually gone
4. **Review** — defend your reasoning in a reviewer session

## Scenario

You're a staff engineer at a personal-finance app with 4 million users across the EU and US. The app aggregates users' bank transactions, categorizes spending, and — the growth team's pride — uses behavioral data to power two features: personalized product recommendations (credit cards, loans, insurance) and an automated "creditworthiness pre-check" that decides which loan offers a user is shown. The company is ad- and referral-funded: partners pay for placement, so the recommendation pipeline ingests everything — transaction history, location from the mobile app, session behavior, and third-party data purchased from a broker. Raw event logs are retained indefinitely; the data warehouse holds 6 years of history and nobody has ever deleted anything.

Three things happened in the past year:

1. **Proxy discrimination in the pre-check model**: a journalist showed that users from certain postal codes were systematically shown worse loan offers. The model never saw ethnicity as a feature, but postal code and merchant patterns acted as close proxies for it — the model had learned and amplified a bias from historical data, exactly the "money laundering for bias" pattern. Affected users had no way to see why they were scored that way and no path to appeal.
2. **A consent complaint under GDPR**: a European regulator opened an inquiry after users complained that the consent flow used a pre-ticked "improve my experience" box that silently enabled sale of behavioral data to the broker, and that the privacy policy obscured rather than explained it. Legal says the consent was likely not "freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous", and that much of the warehouse data fails purpose limitation — it was collected for one thing and explored for another.
3. **A near-miss breach**: an attacker gained read access to a stale analytics replica containing 6 years of transaction histories, including data from users who deleted their accounts years ago. It was caught before exfiltration, but the incident review's conclusion was blunt: most of that data provided no current business value — it was a toxic asset sitting there waiting to leak.

Leadership wants a redesign of how the product collects, retains, uses, and deletes personal data — one that keeps the personalization features viable but survives regulatory scrutiny and treats users as humans rather than metrics. They want to know what you'd stop collecting, what a defensible consent and retention model looks like, how deletion would actually work through the derived datasets, and how you'd make the automated loan pre-check accountable and appealable.

Walk me through how you'd redesign this system's data handling from collection to deletion.
