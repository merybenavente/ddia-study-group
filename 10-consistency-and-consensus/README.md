# Chapter 10 — Consistency and Consensus

**DDIA 2nd Edition, Chapter 10**

> "Never go to sea with two chronometers; take one or three."

## Topics

- Two philosophies for replicated data: eventual consistency (application deals with conflicts, multi-leader/leaderless) vs strong consistency (system behaves as if single-node, at a performance and availability cost)
- Linearizability: making a system appear as if there is only one copy of the data with atomic operations, linearizability as a recency guarantee
  - What makes a system linearizable: registers, read/write/CAS operations, concurrent operations returning old or new values, the constraint that once any read returns the new value all subsequent reads must too, visualizing atomic "take effect" points that must move forward in time, testing linearizability from recorded request timings
  - Linearizability vs serializability: isolation of multi-object transactions vs recency of single-object reads/writes, why serializability permits stale reads, strict serializability (strong-1SR), CockroachDB vs Spanner and FoundationDB, choosing isolation level and consistency model independently
  - Relying on linearizability: locking and leader election (leases, ZooKeeper/etcd, Apache Curator, Oracle RAC page-level locks), constraints and uniqueness guarantees (usernames, account balances, seat booking as lock/CAS analogs), cross-channel timing dependencies (file storage + message queue race in a video transcoder, push notifications hitting lagging replicas)
  - Implementing linearizable systems: single-leader replication (potentially linearizable, delusional leaders violate it), consensus algorithms (likely linearizable — Zab in ZooKeeper, Raft in etcd), multi-leader (not linearizable), leaderless/Dynamo-style (probably not linearizable — LWW with time-of-day clocks, the nonlinearizable quorum execution despite w + r > n, synchronous read repair as a partial fix, why linearizable CAS requires consensus)
  - The cost of linearizability: network partitions forcing a choice between linearizability and availability, the CAP theorem (CP vs AP, its narrow formal scope, "consistent or available when partitioned", why "pick two of three" is misleading, PACELC), linearizability and network delays (even multi-core RAM is not linearizable, the Attiya-Welch result: linearizable response time proportional to network delay uncertainty, performance — not fault tolerance — as the usual reason to drop it)
- ID generators and logical clocks: single-node autoincrementing IDs as a linearizable fetch-and-add, why ID order matters (chat message ordering)
  - Distributed ID generation schemes and their weaker ordering: sharded ID assignment (even/odd, shard bits), preallocated blocks of IDs, random UUIDs (v4), wall-clock timestamps made unique (UUIDv7, X's Snowflake, ULIDs, Flake IDs, MongoDB ObjectIDs) and why clock skew makes them nonlinearizable
  - Logical clocks: counting events rather than measuring time, requirements (compact, unique, totally ordered, consistent with causality)
  - Lamport timestamps: (counter, node ID) pairs, forwarding the counter on message receipt, total order consistent with causality without linearizability
  - Hybrid logical clocks: combining physical time with Lamport-style forwarding, monotonicity despite NTP jumps, use in CockroachDB
  - Lamport/hybrid clocks vs vector clocks: generating MVCC transaction IDs, arbitrary ordering of concurrent timestamps, vector clocks for detecting concurrency and their space cost
  - Linearizable ID generators: the photo-privacy anomaly from nonlinearizable IDs across shards, single-node timestamp oracle (TiDB/TiKV, Google Percolator), batching ID blocks to avoid per-request persistence, Spanner's clock-uncertainty wait as a communication-free alternative, why sharding or geo-distributing the ID generator breaks linearizability
  - Enforcing constraints with logical clocks: why lowest-timestamp-wins fails (you must hear from every node, one unreachable node halts the system) — the need for consensus
- Consensus: getting multiple nodes to agree on a single value, the fundamental problem behind fault-tolerant leader election, ID generation, and CAS
  - The FLP impossibility result: no deterministic algorithm always terminates in the asynchronous model with crash faults, why timeouts and randomness make consensus solvable in practice
  - The many faces of consensus and their equivalence: single-value consensus (uniform agreement, integrity, validity, termination; termination as a liveness property requiring a functioning majority; safety properties held even when a majority fails), CAS as consensus, shared logs / total order broadcast (eventual append, reliable delivery, append-only, agreement, validity), fetch-and-add and its consensus number of 2, atomic commitment (relationship to 2PC, the nontriviality property)
  - Consensus in practice: most systems provide shared logs (Raft, Viewstamped Replication, Zab, Multi-Paxos), state machine replication, shared logs for serializable transactions (deterministic stored procedures), per-shard logs and their cross-shard limits, deriving CAS/seat-booking/fencing tokens (zxid) from a log
  - From single-leader replication to consensus: the chicken-and-egg problem of electing a leader, epoch numbers (ballot/view/term numbers), uniqueness of the leader within an epoch, two rounds of voting (leader election, then per-entry quorum votes) with overlapping quorums, how this differs from 2PC
  - Subtleties: ensuring a new leader honors the old leader's committed entries (Raft's up-to-date requirement vs Paxos's catch-up), weakening consensus for availability (Kafka's unclean leader election), linearizable reads requiring a quorum check (etcd), cluster reconfiguration
  - Pros and cons of consensus: "single-leader replication done right", why homegrown automatic failover is likely unsafe, the strict majority requirement (3 nodes for 1 failure, 5 for 2), no throughput scaling from more nodes, minority partitions blocked, timeout tuning, Raft's leadership-bouncing edge cases and the pre-vote extension, leaderless protocols like Egalitarian Paxos (EPaxos)
- Coordination services: ZooKeeper, etcd, Consul, modeled on Google's Chubby; small in-memory replicated datasets rather than general-purpose storage; Kubernetes on etcd, Spark/Flink on ZooKeeper
  - Core features: locks and leases via fault-tolerant CAS, fencing token support (zxid/cversion in ZooKeeper, revision in etcd), failure detection via sessions and heartbeats (ephemeral nodes), change notifications
  - Uses: configuration management, allocating work to nodes (leader election for job schedulers, shard assignment and rebalancing), outsourcing consensus to a small fixed cluster of 3–5 nodes
  - Service discovery: why it usually doesn't need linearizability, caching and TTLs, DNS-based discovery, ZooKeeper observers (non-voting replicas serving possibly stale reads)

## Assignment

Design a fault-tolerant coordination service — the kind of system that sits underneath a sharded platform and answers questions like "who is the leader for shard 7?", "did this username registration win?", and "what fencing token does this lease-holder carry?". Your service must keep a small amount of critical state replicated across a handful of nodes, survive the crash of any single node (including the leader) without losing committed writes or splitting brain, and offer linearizable operations where they're needed. This chapter's core insight is that leader election, uniqueness constraints, CAS, fencing tokens, and shared logs are all the same underlying problem — consensus — and your design must confront that problem head-on rather than assuming a magic single node.

1. **Theory pass** — conversational interview on linearizability (definition, vs serializability, when it's required), the CAP theorem and the cost of linearizability, logical clocks (Lamport, hybrid, vector) and what ordering they do and don't give you, the equivalence of consensus formulations, epoch numbers and quorum voting, and coordination services
2. **Design pass** — produce a `DESIGN.md`: decide which operations your service must make linearizable and which can be weaker, choose how to formulate the core agreement problem (single-value consensus, CAS, shared log), design leader election with epochs and the quorum rules that prevent split brain, decide how a new leader learns of the old leader's committed entries, define how linearizable reads are served (and what a stale read costs), design fencing token generation, specify behavior on each side of a network partition, and choose an ID/ordering scheme for the state you manage. Include rejected alternatives with reasoning.
3. **Implementation pass** — build a working prototype (simulated cluster of nodes exchanging messages) that demonstrates: replicated writes confirmed by a quorum, automatic leader failover that preserves committed entries, split-brain prevention under a simulated network partition, a linearizable operation (e.g., unique-name registration or lease acquisition) racing across concurrent clients, monotonic fencing tokens, and behavior under simulated faults (leader crash, partition, delayed and reordered messages)
4. **Review** — defend your reasoning in a reviewer session

## Scenario

You're a senior engineer at a live-events ticketing platform. The system sells about 8 million tickets per month across 3 regions, and big on-sales (a stadium tour going live at 10:00 AM) spike to 30,000 requests per second for a few minutes. Seat inventory is sharded by event, each shard has a designated "seat allocator" node, and purchases flow through a message queue to downstream services that generate ticket PDFs and send confirmations.

The current architecture grew organically: seat state lives in a Dynamo-style store with quorum reads and writes, shard leadership is managed by a homegrown failover script, and order IDs come from wall-clock timestamps. Three incidents in the past two quarters have shaken confidence:

1. **Double-sold seats**: during a major on-sale, two buyers were both confirmed for the same seat. The team believed quorum reads and writes (w + r > n) gave them "strong consistency," but the store uses last-write-wins with time-of-day timestamps, and even its quorums permit nonlinearizable executions — one buyer's read saw the seat as free after another buyer's confirmation had completed. Seat allocation is a uniqueness constraint, and enforcing it at write time requires linearizability — effectively an atomic CAS on the seat, which a leaderless quorum system cannot provide.
2. **Split-brain seat allocators**: an inter-AZ network fault triggered the failover script, which promoted a new allocator for a shard while the old one was alive but unreachable. Both nodes handed out seats and order IDs for 90 seconds. The script had no notion of epoch numbers and no quorum vote — exactly the homegrown automatic failover the chapter warns is likely unsafe without a proven consensus algorithm.
3. **Phantom tickets**: after a purchase, the confirmation message on the queue reached the PDF-generation service before the order record had replicated in the storage service. The generator read a stale replica, found nothing, and emailed customers broken tickets. A classic cross-channel timing dependency: two communication channels (queue and storage) with no recency guarantee between them.

Leadership has approved a redesign of the coordination and consistency layer: seat allocation, shard leadership, and order ID generation. They want to know precisely which operations need linearizability and what that costs during network partitions and cross-region traffic — and they never want a double-sold seat or a second allocator again.

Walk me through how you'd design the coordination service and consistency guarantees for this platform.
